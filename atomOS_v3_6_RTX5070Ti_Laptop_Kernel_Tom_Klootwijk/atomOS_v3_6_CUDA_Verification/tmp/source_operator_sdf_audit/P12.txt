The Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP) is one of the most famous and intensively 
studied NP-hard problems in computer science and combinatorial optimization. [1, 2] 
At its core, the problem asks: Given a list of cities and the distances between each pair, 
what is the absolute shortest possible route that visits every city exactly once and 
returns to the starting city? [1] 
Here is a breakdown of why it is so difficult, how it is classified, and how we solve it. 
 
    Why is TSP so hard? 
The main reason TSP is NP-hard is combinatorial explosion. As you add more cities, 
the number of possible routes grows factorially. [1, 2, 3] 
For an un-directed graph with n cities, the number of unique tours is: 
\(\frac{(n-1)!}{2}\) 
• For 5 cities, there are 12 possible routes. 
• For 10 cities, there are 181,440 routes. 
• For 20 cities, the count jumps to roughly 1.2 quintillion (1.2 × 10¹⁷) routes. 
• For 50 cities, calculating all options would take a modern supercomputer 
billions of years. [1, 2]

     How Do We Solve It? 
Because finding an exact solution for huge datasets is practically impossible, 
researchers use different tiers of algorithms depending on how much time they have 
and how accurate they need to be. [1, 2] 
1. Exact Algorithms (Guaranteed Best Route, But Slow) 
• Brute Force: Checking every path. Practically useless for n > 20. 
• Held-Karp (Dynamic Programming): Reduces complexity to \(O(2^n \cdot 
n^2)\). It handles a few dozen cities but still hits an exponential wall. 
• Branch and Bound: Discards paths that are already known to be worse than the 
current best option. This has allowed researchers to solve specific instances 
with tens of thousands of cities. [1, 2, 3, 4] 
2. Approximation & Heuristic Algorithms (Fast, But Near-Optimal) 
In the real world, a route that is 1% longer than perfect but takes 2 seconds to calculate 
is much better than a perfect route that takes 100 years to compute. [1, 2] 
• Nearest Neighbor Heuristic: The salesman simply goes to the closest unvisited 
city next. It is incredibly fast but can result in highly suboptimal routes if the last 
remaining cities are far away. [1] 
• 2-Opt Local Search: Starts with a random route and systematically uncrosses 
paths ("swapping" edges) to iteratively shorten the loop. [1] 
• Christofides Algorithm: A specialized math framework for "Metric TSPs" (where 
distances follow the triangle inequality) that guarantees the solution will be no 
worse than 1.5 times the optimal route. [1] 
• Metaheuristics: Genetic Algorithms, Simulated Annealing, and Ant Colony 
Optimization use nature-inspired randomness to "evolve" or hunt down 
incredibly efficient routes for millions of cities within a tiny fraction of 1% of the 
perfect answer. [1]
